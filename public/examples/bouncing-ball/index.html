Infinitely Many Bounces

A ball keeps a fixed fraction of its speed at every bounce. So it never has a last bounce — there is always another, smaller one. And yet it stops, at a moment you can calculate exactly.

Where the paradox goes. Each bounce returns the ball to e² of its previous height, so the apexes fall away as a geometric sequence and there is no final bounce. The times shrink geometrically too, and a geometric series converges — so the infinite sequence of bounces is crammed into a finite total, T = √(2h₀/g) · (1+e)/(1−e). Nothing here is approximated: the number in comes to rest at is that formula, and the animation is drawn from the same closed form.

Why the apexes are exact. Each flight is drawn as a true parabola with its bounce time solved for directly, rather than by stepping a simulation forward. A fixed-step integrator detects the floor a fraction of a step late, quietly steals energy, and drifts below the theoretical apexes — about 0.1 % per bounce here, which is small until you are trying to demonstrate a precise claim about heights. Measured against theory, the apexes on screen agree to every digit shown.

Try e near the extremes. At e = 0.9 the ball is still visibly hopping long after it should have settled, and the rest time stretches out sharply — the 1/(1−e) factor blows up as e approaches 1. A perfectly elastic ball would never stop at all.
