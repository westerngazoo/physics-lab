The abstraction — four devices, one equation, two families

Real means light actually arrives there — the refracted rays genuinely cross, and you could put a screen at that spot and see the picture. It lands on the far side of the lens, and it is upside down.

Virtual means the light never converges. It leaves the lens still spreading apart, and your eye traces it back to a place the light only seems to come from — dashed, because nothing is there. No screen will catch it. It sits on the same side as the object, and it stays upright.

Why the glass gets fatter. Drag the focal-length slider and the lens changes shape, because those are the same fact: the lensmaker's relation 1/f = (n−1)(1/R1 − 1/R2) ties focal length to how sharply the surfaces curve. Short focal length means strongly curved, fat glass; long means nearly flat. The outline here is indicative — the thin-lens model still does all its bending at the centre plane, which is why the rays kink there rather than at the two surfaces.

The real category is the sign of f. A convex lens and a concave mirror share the same five cases; a concave lens and a convex mirror share the same single one. Whether light bends through glass or bounces off silver barely matters — both obey 1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ, and the sign of f decides everything about the case structure. The only trait the kind owns is geography: a lens forms its real images on the far side (light passes through), a mirror on the object’s own side (light comes back). The traits table above is generated from the same data the solver runs on, so it cannot drift from the model.

A diverging element — concave lens or convex mirror — can only ever make the virtual kind of image. Sweep the object anywhere: virtual, upright, reduced, every time. That is why its case strip has just one zone, and why shop-security mirrors (convex) show a small upright world regardless of where anything stands.
